Where it breaks

See the waves before you paddle out.

Every daylight hour at every break comes with a map of where the waves are expected to break. StokeRanker follows the forecast swell in over a laser survey of the seabed, so you can see where it peels, which way, how far you’d ride, and where it closes out.

Same swell. Different beach.

One swell, two stories.

Tuesday at 9am, the same 6-second south-easterly reaches both beaches. At Mullaway it bends around the rocky point, and rights peel off it while the beach beside it closes out. Park Beach is open sand: the map shows where it breaks, but its banks move after every big swell, so it doesn’t guess which way they peel.

The physics

Real wave physics, on the real seabed.

A swell forecast tells you what’s arriving out to sea. StokeRanker follows each wave in to the sand with refraction ray tracing, a standard tool of coastal engineering, run over an airborne laser survey of every break’s seabed, from Port Macquarie to Tweed Heads. It’s the same physics that makes a point break work, run for every break, every hour.

  1. 1

    Start with the forecast swell.

    Each hour’s main swell, its period and the direction it comes from, is taken from the marine forecast, out in deep water.

  2. 2

    Trace it over the seabed.

    Wave rays are traced in across a 5 m grid of the NSW Marine LiDAR seabed survey. Waves slow down in shallow water, so they bend towards banks and reefs, growing where they bunch up and shrinking where they spread out.

  3. 3

    Find where it breaks, and how.

    Where the water gets shallow enough, the waves break. A crest that meets the bank at an angle peels; one that hits it square on closes out. The map shades each stretch by how well it peels for your kind of surfing, fades it where the waves are small or few, and shows the way it peels and how far you’d ride.

Reading the map

Blue peels. Hatched closes out.

The band is where surfable waves break, from where the biggest sets first break to where most waves are whitewater.

  • Solid blue it peels
  • Light blue it peels in sections, between close-outs
  • Hatched orange it closes out
  • Dotted purple it breaks on a beach break, where the sand banks aren’t known
  • Paler smaller or fewer waves
  • →Arrow the way it peels, with the ride length
  • Ring the take-off the score is worked out for

A line across the top sums it up, and the swell arrow shows where the swell comes from, its height, and its period. Colours are from the Okabe-Ito colour-blind-safe palette, with hatching and dots so no meaning rests on colour alone.

The physics view

Want the working?

The physics view shows what the map is drawn from: the traced wave crests, the depths, both take-offs, and the numbers behind it. On the web, tap Physics view under the map. In the app, turn your phone sideways.

  • White lines wave crests, one wave period apart
  • Dashed line where the biggest sets break, about 1 in 100 waves
  • Thin line where half the waves have broken
  • ☆Star the first surfable section the seabed trace finds

Honest about it

A forecast, not a photo.

Sand moves

Banks shift.

The seabed survey is from 2018, and sand banks move after every big swell. So on beach breaks the map shows where waves break but makes no call on peel.

Simplified

Some things it can’t see.

It doesn’t model waves wrapping into the lee of a headland, or currents, so sheltered corners can show less than turns up.

Checked

Held up against the beach.

Photos taken on the beach are marked up and compared with the maps, and the width of the surf zone is checked against satellite passes.
